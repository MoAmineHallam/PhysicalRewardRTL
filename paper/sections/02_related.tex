\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

We organise related work into three strands: LLM-based generation of RTL,
optimisation of RTL for physical objectives, and the evaluation practices that
this work departs from. Table~\ref{tab:delta} summarises the position of the
present paper with respect to the most closely related contributions.

\subsection{Language models for RTL generation}

The first strand treats hardware description as a code-generation problem.
Benchmarks such as VerilogEval~\cite{liu2023verilogeval} and
RTLLM~\cite{lu2024rtllm} established pass@$k$ on small, self-contained module
specifications as the standard measure, and a series of models has advanced that
measure through domain-specific pre-training and instruction tuning:
VeriGen~\cite{thakur2024verigen} fine-tunes on a curated Verilog corpus,
RTLCoder~\cite{liu2024rtlcoder} constructs an automated instruction dataset and
trains a compact open model competitive with much larger general-purpose ones,
CodeV~\cite{zhao2024codev} exploits multi-level summarisation to build training
pairs, and CraftRTL~\cite{liu2024craftrtl} addresses non-textual representations
and targeted repair of known failure modes. More recently, reinforcement
learning has been applied to the same objective:
VeriSeek~\cite{wang2024veriseek} uses reinforcement learning with a
verification-derived signal, and VeriReason~\cite{wang2025verireason} combines
testbench feedback with structured reasoning to improve functional pass rates.

What unites this strand is the objective. In every case the reward or the metric
is functional: does the module compile, and does it match a reference on a test
suite? Physical quality is either unmeasured or reported as an
afterthought. Our work adopts the same starting point---we begin from
RTLCoder~\cite{liu2024rtlcoder} and additionally verify transfer to
Qwen2.5-Coder~\cite{hui2024qwen25coder}---but replaces the objective with a
physical one that is gated by, rather than equal to, functional correctness.

\subsection{Optimising RTL for physical objectives}

The second strand does target power, performance and area (PPA), and has become
active very recently. REvolution~\cite{min2025revolution} couples an LLM with an
evolutionary search that maintains separate populations for repairing failures
and improving PPA. POET~\cite{poet2026} applies evolutionary tuning with an
explicit power orientation, EvolVE~\cite{evolve2026} performs evolutionary search
over Verilog generation and optimisation, COEVO~\cite{coevo2026} co-evolves
functional correctness and PPA, and Dr.~RTL~\cite{drrtl2026} performs agentic,
tool-grounded self-improvement of RTL. RTLRewriter~\cite{yao2024rtlrewriter}
approaches the problem from the rewriting rather than the generation side.
On the evaluation side, RTL-OPT~\cite{lu2026rtlopt} contributes a benchmark of
$36$ hand-crafted designs paired with human-optimised references specifically to
measure optimisation quality rather than syntactic correctness, covering
combinational logic, pipelined datapaths, finite state machines and memory
interfaces.

Two differences separate these approaches from ours, and both are consequential.
The first is \emph{where the optimisation lives}. Evolutionary and agentic
methods search at inference time: they generate many candidates and select or
mutate among them, so the cost of a good design is paid on every invocation. Our
method moves the optimisation into the policy, so a single sample from the
trained model is already the fast design; Section~\ref{sec:bestofn} quantifies
this difference against an idealised best-of-$N$ selector and shows that one
sample from our policy exceeds a \emph{perfectly selected} best of forty-eight
supervised samples in the interpolation regime. The second difference is that
these methods optimise PPA without a hard correctness precondition on the
reward, which is precisely the configuration in which a physical objective is
exploitable; Section~\ref{sec:hacking} documents what that exploitation looks
like when it occurs.

\subsection{High-level synthesis and learned PPA prediction}

Commercial high-level synthesis (HLS) is the established route from an
algorithmic description to RTL, and any claim about automatically generated
accelerators must be positioned against it. We therefore compare directly
against Vitis HLS in two configurations---without directives, as a non-expert
would use it, and with hand-placed pipeline, unroll and array-partition
pragmas---in Section~\ref{sec:hls}. Learned models for predicting post-synthesis
quality from source-level features have been studied for HLS
designs~\cite{ustun2020accurate,wu2022highlevel}, and our frequency surrogate is
a deliberately simple member of this family; its role and its limitations are
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:surrogate}.

\subsection{Evaluation practice}

The third strand is methodological. Contamination between training and
evaluation data is a recognised hazard in code generation generally, and the RTL
literature is particularly exposed to it because parameterised design families
make it easy to train on a $16$-tap filter and evaluate on an $18$-tap one
without noticing that the two share an implementation template. Reported
results in this area are also predominantly based on synthesis or timing-analysis
estimates rather than measurement on a device. We depart from prevailing
practice in three specific ways: a held-out split frozen before training and
enforced programmatically, an explicit separation of interpolation from
extrapolation, and validation of the headline claim on a physical device with a
co-resident canary that bounds attribution. We do not claim to be the first to
place LLM-generated RTL on an FPGA; we claim that the attribution methodology
described in Section~\ref{sec:silicon} is more explicit than what is currently
reported.
