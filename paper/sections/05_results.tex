\section{Results}

\subsection{Supervised warm start}
\label{sec:warmstart}

Table~\ref{tab:sft} reports oracle correctness before and after supervised
fine-tuning, measured over $16$ samples per design. The un-tuned base models
produce correct accelerators in $1.9$--$6.9\%$ of samples, confirming that a

fine-tuning the five-family model reaches $94.8\%$ overall. The recipe is
unchanged between the two base models, and the resulting competence is

Correctness is preserved rather than traded away in aggregate, but not uniformly.
Several designs improve markedly (\texttt{fir26} $79 \rightarrow 96\%$,

in common.

\subsection{Mechanism: reallocation, not invention}
\label{sec:mechanism}

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} plots every distinct correct implementation produced
by the supervised model and by the optimised policy, with marker area
proportional to how many of the $48$ samples collapsed onto it. The pattern is
consistent across all fifteen designs shown: the supervised model scatters,
producing a cluster of slow implementations with an occasional fast outlier,
while the optimised policy concentrates almost all of its mass on the fast
implementation.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_mechanism}
\caption{Per-candidate real \fmax{} for each held-out design. The supervised
model (circles) produces a slow cluster with occasional fast outliers; the
optimised policy (diamonds) concentrates on the fast style. Marker area is
proportional to the number of samples producing that implementation.}
\label{fig:mechanism}
\end{figure*}

Individual designs make the point concretely. For \texttt{fir36} the supervised
samples measure $\{17, 188, 23, 23\}$~MHz while the optimised samples measure
$\{188, 188, 188\}$; for \texttt{poly7} the supervised samples measure
$\{33,33,33,33,33,191\}$ against $\{192,192,192\}$. In both cases the maximum
is essentially unchanged---the supervised model \emph{can} write the fast
design---but its frequency of doing so changes from roughly one sample in six to
near-certainty.

This supports a precise and deliberately limited claim: \textbf{the method
reallocates probability mass onto the fastest implementation style the policy
already possesses; it does not discover implementations faster than those in the
corpus.} The ceiling is set by the supervised distribution, and the optimiser
determines how reliably that ceiling is reached. Two designs marginally exceed
the supervised maximum (\texttt{firr10} $334$ versus $313$~MHz, \texttt{firr40}
$195$ versus $189$), which we attribute to minor structural variation rather
than to a qualitatively new implementation.

The same claim predicts where the method should fail, and it does so correctly.
The \texttt{iir} family gains only $10$--$25\%$ because its supervised
distribution already emits fast forms frequently, leaving little slow tail to
remove; its ceiling is bounded by the loop-carried dependency and is unchanged.
The \texttt{med} family gains nothing at all: the compact behavioural style that
made this family \emph{correct} is fully combinational by construction, so the
policy possesses no fast median implementation to amplify. Predicting one's own
negative results is a stronger test of a mechanistic claim than confirming one's
positive ones.

\subsection{Comparison against idealised best-of-$N$}
\label{sec:bestofn}

The best-of-eight column of Table~\ref{tab:money} depends on the quality of the
selector. To remove that dependence we compute the expected best-of-$N$ under a
\emph{perfect} selector in closed form from the empirical per-design
distribution, which upper-bounds any reranking scheme including an oracle-quality
one. Figure~\ref{fig:bestofn} shows the resulting curves.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_bestofn}
\caption{Expected \fmax{} of the best of $N$ supervised samples under a
\emph{perfect} selector, against a single sample from the optimised policy.
Under interpolation one optimised sample exceeds a perfectly selected best of
forty-eight supervised samples.}
\label{fig:bestofn}
\end{figure}

Under interpolation, a single sample from the optimised policy ($222.6$~MHz)
exceeds the expected best of \emph{forty-eight} perfectly selected supervised
samples ($207.8$~MHz); under extrapolation it is equivalent to a perfectly
selected best of thirty-two. The comparison is conservative in the policy's
disfavour, since the perfect selector is unattainable in practice.

More decisively, sampling cannot solve the problem at all on some designs. For
\texttt{firr10} the supervised model does not produce the fast implementation in
any of forty-eight samples ($68.9$~MHz at $N=48$, against $306.8$~MHz for the
policy), and for \texttt{fir40} the corresponding figures are $20.9$ and
$181.8$~MHz. These are existence failures rather than selection failures: no
selector can choose an implementation that was never generated. This
distinguishes distribution shift from improved sampling, and is the clearest
evidence that the optimisation occurs in the policy rather than in the
selection.

Three methodological points govern how these numbers should be read. First, the
comparison is \emph{within} a bitstream: both members of a pair are placed and
routed together, so the difference cannot be attributed to build-to-build
variation. Second, a canary of independently characterised frequency is
co-resident, and its margin ($159.1$~MHz above the highest reported entry at
harness validation) bounds what may be attributed to the designs; two optimised
entries reach the harness ceiling of $200$~MHz and are therefore reported as

silicon and STA at harness validation, and $125$ versus $193$--$228$~MHz for the
in-context versus standalone builds of the same filters), because an in-context
build shares the fabric with the harness. We report the in-context numbers
because they are what was measured, and note the discrepancy rather than
selecting the more favourable figure.

The transfer succeeds, but the more informative observation is the coincidence of
the absolute values. The two base models differ substantially in starting
point---base correctness $6.9\%$ against $1.9\%$, post-supervision $74.4\%$
against $68.1\%$, and different weak families---yet their optimised policies
converge to $232.9$ and $190.1$~MHz against $234.4$ and $190.1$~MHz for the
first model. Since the frequency ceiling is a property of the design and the
device rather than of the generator, this is what one would expect if the method
reliably locates that ceiling, and it corroborates the mechanism of
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} from an independent direction.

A secondary observation is that policy optimisation \emph{repaired} this model's
weakest family: held-out \texttt{poly7} correctness rose from $29$--$54\%$ under
supervision to $85$--$100\%$ after optimisation, while simultaneously reaching
$191$~MHz. Because reward is accessible only through correctness, pressure toward
frequency also creates pressure toward correctness on designs where the policy
was previously unreliable.

\subsection{Reward hacking: detection and a caught case}
\label{sec:hacking}

Introducing two structurally new circuit classes degraded the surrogate. Trained
on the three multiply--accumulate families it achieved a leave-one-design-out
Spearman correlation of $0.965$ with top-1 selection accuracy $23/25$; retrained
across five families the pooled correlation fell to $0.75$--$0.82$ (the range
reflects seed-to-seed variation on identical data, which is itself worth
reporting) with top-1 accuracy $17$--$18/22$. Text-derived features that capture
what limits a multiply--accumulate chain do not capture what limits a feedback
loop or a comparator network.

The consequence is visible in Figure~\ref{fig:saturation}. Designs whose
surrogate score saturated at the clamp boundary of $500$~MHz suffered
correctness regressions, while designs with informative surrogate scores held or
improved. Saturation is therefore an \emph{observable} early-warning signal for
reward hacking, available during training without any synthesis.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_saturation}
\caption{Correctness change against the optimised policy's surrogate score.
Designs whose surrogate saturated at the clamp (crosses) are those that lost
correctness; designs with informative surrogate values held or improved.}
\label{fig:saturation}
\end{figure}

The clearest case is the median family. The surrogate reported $188.6$~MHz for
optimised \texttt{med7} designs; the same designs measure $36$--$40$~MHz in
timing-closed synthesis. Had we reported surrogate values, this family would have
appeared as the method's most dramatic success---a $4.5\times$ improvement---when
in fact it achieved no frequency gain whatsoever. This is the concrete
justification for the invariant of Section~\ref{sec:surrogate}.

Saturation does not always indicate hacking, and distinguishing the two cases
requires measurement. The polynomial family also saturated the surrogate, but
its designs genuinely measure $191$--$193$~MHz: there the surrogate was wrong in
magnitude while remaining correct in \emph{ranking}, which is sufficient for
group-relative advantage estimation. Saturation is thus a signal to verify, not
a verdict.

The geometric mean of our throughput against expert HLS is $0.96\times$: starting

\subsection{Comparison against a frontier API model}
\label{sec:frontier}

We submitted the identical held-out prompts to a frontier commercial model in two
configurations: \emph{plain}, the exact prompt our models receive, and
\emph{fast}, the same prompt with an explicit instruction to maximise clock
frequency and pipeline aggressively. Both were scored by the same oracle and
synthesised by the same flow. The second configuration is included because
omitting it would invite the assumption that a frontier model would succeed if
only it were asked properly.

Under the identical prompt, the frontier model produces slow RTL---$20.7$~MHz for
\texttt{fir40}, $32.1$ for \texttt{fir26}, $70.4$ for \texttt{firr10} against
$181.8$, $193.2$ and $313.3$~MHz for our policy, a factor of $2$ to $8.8$. A
frontier model does not emit high-frequency hardware by default.

When explicitly instructed to optimise for frequency, however, it matches or
exceeds our policy's peak frequency on ten of twelve \texttt{fir}/\texttt{firr}
designs by $5$--$30\%$. We report this without qualification. Three measured
factors bound it. Coverage: the model fails almost entirely outside the
multiply--accumulate families, scoring $0\%$ correct on nine of ten held-out
polynomial designs, $0$--$12.5\%$ on recursive filters and $0$--$25\%$ on median
filters, where our policy is $94$--$100\%$ correct across all five families.
Reliability: even where it is fast, it is correct in only $25$--$87.5\%$ of
samples, and which samples are correct is knowable only because our oracle
determines it. Cost: its fast designs purchase frequency with registers---$185$
flip-flops against our $96$ for \texttt{fir6}, and $1410$ against $640$ for
\texttt{fir40}---which is additional latency and area.

The defensible claim is therefore not that our model is faster than a frontier
model, but that a small locally-deployable model is \emph{reliably} correct and
fast across all five families where a much larger model prompted for speed is
neither, and that the oracle that makes such a comparison measurable is itself
part of the contribution.

\subsection{Capability compression}
\label{sec:distill}

oracle-verified correct outputs, and deduplicated, yielding $407$ rows from

Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}. A Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B student was trained on this
corpus with the unchanged recipe in twenty-five minutes, against approximately

\subsection{Negative results}
\label{sec:negative}

We report three negative results in full, because each constrains the claims
above.

\paragraph{A capability ceiling at 7B} The \texttt{cordic} family was constructed,
verified and included in the supervised corpus, and neither base model produced a
single correct implementation in any sample after fine-tuning ($0\%$ on both).
The failure replicates across two independent base models, indicating a
capability-class limit rather than a training artefact. The family is excluded
from policy optimisation for this reason.

\paragraph{A capability--size frontier at 1.5B} The median family reaches $89.6\%$
correctness at 6.7B and $0\%$ at 1.5B (Table~\ref{tab:distill}), with $4$--$10$ of
$16$ samples compiling but none correct. The same pattern as \texttt{cordic},
one size class lower.

\paragraph{No frequency gain on comparator networks} The median family gained
correctness ($79 \rightarrow 85\%$ interpolation, $29 \rightarrow 40\%$
extrapolation) but no frequency ($36$--$40$~MHz before and after). The cause is
structural and was diagnosed rather than merely observed: the compact behavioural
sorting style that made this family correct in the first place is fully
combinational, so its critical path is long by construction and the policy has no
fast alternative to amplify. The remedy is a pipelined median template in the
corpus, which is future work; the point here is that the mechanism of
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} predicts this outcome rather than being embarrassed
by it.
