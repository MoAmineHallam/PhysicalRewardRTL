\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the results support, and what they do not}

The results of Section~\ref{sec:results} support a narrower claim than a reader
scanning only Table~\ref{tab:money} might infer, and we state the narrow version
explicitly because it is the one that survives scrutiny.

The method \emph{reliably converts a policy that occasionally writes fast
hardware into one that almost always does}, for design families in which a fast
implementation style exists within the supervised distribution. It does not
synthesise implementation styles that were absent from that distribution, and its
achievable ceiling is consequently bounded by the corpus rather than by the
optimiser. Every result in this paper is consistent with that statement,
including the two families where the gain is small or absent, and including the
observation that two different base models converge to the same absolute
frequencies.

This has a practical corollary worth stating for anyone reproducing the method:
effort spent broadening the \emph{style} diversity of the supervised corpus is
likely to raise the ceiling more than effort spent on the reinforcement learning
stage, which principally affects how reliably that ceiling is reached. The
median family is the clean demonstration---no amount of policy optimisation can
extract frequency from a family whose only reliably-emitted style is
combinational.

\subsection{Threats to validity}

\paragraph{Domain scope} We evaluate single-input streaming DSP accelerators with
a uniform interface. This is a deliberate restriction, and it is what makes the
per-design oracle and the uniform hardware harness feasible; the rigour of the
evaluation and the narrowness of the domain are not independent choices. We
therefore make no claim about general RTL generation, and specifically no claim
about control-dominated logic, memory interfaces or multi-clock designs. Whether
the method transfers to those classes is open, and the surrogate would certainly
require different features to do so.

\paragraph{Template-generated specifications} Both the specifications and the
supervised completions are generated programmatically from templates. This
guarantees oracle-verifiable correctness and eliminates contamination from
scraped code, but it means the natural-language inputs are more uniform than a
practitioner's prose would be. The extrapolation regime (parameters outside the
trained grid, and coefficient variants constructed only at evaluation time) is
our attempt to test style transfer beyond memorisation, but it does not test
robustness to linguistic variation in the specification.

\paragraph{Single device family} All synthesis and all hardware measurements
target one FPGA (Zynq-7020). Frequency ceilings are device-specific, and the
absolute values reported here would differ on another part. The relative
comparisons are internally consistent because every policy is measured under
identical conditions, but we do not claim device independence.

\paragraph{Sample sizes} Held-out evaluation uses $n=48$ samples per design with
two oracle seeds at length $1024$; the frontier-model comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:frontier} uses $n=8$ per arm, where a single sample shifts a
reported percentage by $12.5$ points, and should be read as indicative. The
surrogate's leave-one-design-out correlation varies by approximately $0.07$
between random seeds on identical data, so small differences in that statistic
carry little information.

\paragraph{Area and latency} The optimised designs are generally smaller in
look-up tables while consuming more flip-flops, since pipelining trades registers
for frequency; deeper pipelines also increase latency, which is immaterial for
streaming throughput but not for latency-sensitive use. Designs containing
multipliers map to DSP blocks, so look-up-table counts alone understate resource
use, and we report DSP counts alongside them.

\subsection{On surrogate-guided physical optimisation}

The reward hacking documented in Section~\ref{sec:hacking} is, in our view, the
most transferable finding of this work, because any attempt to optimise a
physical objective with a learned proxy will encounter it. Three observations
generalise beyond our setting.

First, hacking was detectable without synthesis: saturation at the clamp boundary
separated the degraded designs from the healthy ones. A practitioner can monitor
this during training at no cost.

Second, the clamp was necessary but not sufficient. It converted an unbounded
exploit (numerical overflow, effectively infinite reward) into a bounded one
(a $500$~MHz ceiling), which preserved training stability but did not prevent the
policy from optimising against a fiction on the family where the surrogate was
blind.

Third, saturation is a signal to verify rather than a verdict. The polynomial
family saturated the surrogate and was genuinely fast; the median family
saturated it and was not. Only measurement distinguishes them, which is why the
invariant that surrogate values never appear in results is load-bearing rather
than stylistic. A method that reported its own reward as its result would have
claimed a $4.5\times$ improvement on a family that improved by nothing.

\subsection{Cost}

The complete pipeline for one base model comprises approximately three hours of
supervised fine-tuning, roughly seven hours of policy optimisation on a single
V100-class device, and a Vivado pass over the evaluation candidates. The
surrogate is trained on $177$ labelled pairs, each requiring one synthesis run.
Distillation into the 1.5B student adds twenty-five minutes. The dominant cost is
not the reinforcement learning but the electronic design automation runs required
to produce trustworthy labels---which is itself an argument for the surrogate,
and for re-anchoring it periodically against real measurements rather than
enlarging it.
