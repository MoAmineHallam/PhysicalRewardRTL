\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The design of digital hardware begins with a register-transfer level (RTL)
description, and writing that description is still a slow, expert-intensive
activity. It is therefore unsurprising that the rapid progress of large language
models (LLMs) on software source code has been followed by a substantial body of
work on generating Verilog and VHDL automatically. That body of work has, almost
without exception, adopted \emph{functional correctness} as its objective: a
generated module is scored by whether it compiles and whether it reproduces the
outputs of a reference on a testbench, and progress is reported as a pass rate
on a benchmark suite of small design problems.

Functional correctness is a necessary target, but it is not a sufficient one.
Hardware is not merely required to compute the right function; it is required to
compute the right function fast enough, in a small enough area, on a specific
device. A $40$-tap finite impulse response (FIR) filter written as a single
combinational multiply--accumulate chain and the same filter written as a
transposed, fully pipelined datapath are indistinguishable to a correctness
oracle---they produce identical output streams---yet on a Xilinx Zynq-7020 the
former closes timing at $21$~MHz and the latter at $182$~MHz. A generator that
is evaluated only on correctness is therefore free to emit the $21$~MHz design
indefinitely, and in our experiments a supervised model does exactly that: it
emits the fast implementation style in roughly one sample out of four to eight,
and the slow style the rest of the time. The engineering value of the tool
collapses accordingly, because a designer who cannot tell which sample is fast
must synthesise all of them.

Optimising for the physical objective directly is difficult for three reasons,
each of which has shaped the method proposed here. First, the objective is
expensive: obtaining a true maximum operating frequency (\fmax) requires a full
synthesis and place-and-route pass, which takes minutes per candidate and cannot
be placed inside a reinforcement learning loop that consumes thousands of
candidates. Second, the objective is dangerous to approximate, because any
learned surrogate for \fmax{} becomes an optimisation target that the policy will
exploit; we observed this failure twice, in one instance a policy driving a
surrogate to numerical overflow and in another obtaining a surrogate score of
$188.6$~MHz for a design whose measured frequency is $36$--$40$~MHz. Third, and
most importantly, the objective is trivially satisfiable by an incorrect design.
A module that emits a constant closes timing at an arbitrarily high frequency.
Speed and correctness cannot be traded off against one another with a weighted
sum, because any positive weight on speed makes degenerate designs attractive.

This paper proposes a method that addresses all three difficulties with a single
structural decision. We define a \emph{correctness-gated} reward: the reward of a
generated module is its surrogate-predicted \fmax{} if an input/output
equivalence oracle certifies the module correct, and exactly zero otherwise. The
gate is not a penalty term and not a soft constraint; correctness is a
precondition for receiving any reward at all, so a policy cannot purchase
frequency with correctness. The surrogate makes the reward cheap enough for
online policy optimisation, the oracle makes it safe, and---critically---no
number produced by the surrogate is ever reported as a result. Every frequency
in this paper is either a WNS-derived Vivado result or a clock sweep measured
on a physical device.

We further argue that the evaluation protocol matters as much as the training
method, and that the prevailing protocol in this literature is too weak to
support its conclusions. Reporting a mean speed-up over designs that appeared in
the training corpus measures memorisation, not capability. We therefore freeze a
held-out split \emph{before} any model in this paper was trained, exclude those
designs from the supervised corpus, from the reinforcement learning design list,
and from the surrogate's training rows, and enforce the exclusion with a
programmatic guard that refuses to run if a held-out design appears. The split
distinguishes two regimes that are usually conflated: \emph{interpolation},
where the held-out parameter lies inside the trained range, and
\emph{extrapolation}, where it lies outside it. Results are reported separately
for the two, because they answer different questions.

Our contributions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A correctness-gated physical reward for RTL generation.} We
formulate policy optimisation in which the reward is a physical quantity
(predicted \fmax) gated by an input/output-equivalence oracle, so that
correctness is a precondition rather than a competing objective
(Section~\ref{sec:method}). Applied to five accelerator families spanning three
circuit classes, the method raises mean WNS-derived \fmax{} on thirty frozen
held-out designs from $86.5$ to $211.3$~MHz ($+144\%$) under interpolation and
from $78.5$ to $174.2$~MHz ($+122\%$) under extrapolation, exceeding a
perfect-selector best-of-eight baseline in both regimes.

\item \textbf{Silicon validation with explicit attribution.} Five held-out design
pairs were synthesised into a single bitstream, deployed to a Xilinx Zynq-7020,
and swept over clock frequency in hardware. The optimised designs sustain
$2.25$--$3.40\times$ the measured clock of the supervised baseline, with zero
spread over repeated sweeps, and a canary design of known frequency co-resident
in the same bitstream bounds what may legitimately be attributed to the design
under test (Section~\ref{sec:silicon}).

\item \textbf{A mechanism, measured rather than asserted.} Per-candidate analysis
shows that the method does not invent faster hardware; it reallocates
probability mass onto the fastest implementation style the policy already
possesses. The supervised model emits the fast style in one sample out of four
to eight, the optimised policy emits it almost deterministically, and the
achievable ceiling is set by the corpus rather than by the optimiser
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). This claim also predicts, correctly, the two
families in which our method yields no frequency gain.

\item \textbf{A caught case of reward hacking, and the limits of detecting it.}
Adding structurally dissimilar circuit classes costs the learned frequency
surrogate remarkably little by the conventional offline measure
(leave-one-design-out Spearman $0.972 \rightarrow 0.915$), and yet on $25$ of
$30$ held-out designs it saturates at its clamp while true frequencies span
$36$--$193$~MHz, and in a fully worked case it reports $188.6$~MHz for a median
filter measuring $36$--$40$~MHz. A predictor can retain $\rho > 0.9$ while being
wrong by a factor of five on the designs that matter; the offline number does
not see the failure. We also report a negative methodological result:
saturation does \emph{not} predict correctness degradation in our data, so it
should be treated as a trigger to verify rather than as a diagnosis
(Section~\ref{sec:hacking}).

\item \textbf{Transfer, compression, and honest negative results.} The recipe
transfers unmodified to a second base model, and both models converge to
the same measured frequency ceiling ($\approx 233$ and $\approx 190$~MHz in the
two regimes), suggesting the optimisation recovers a property of the design
space rather than of the model. The learned capability compresses into a
$1.5$~billion-parameter student for four of five families in twenty-five minutes
of training, while the fifth fails entirely---a capability--size frontier that we
report rather than omit, alongside two further negative results
(Sections~\ref{sec:transfer}--\ref{sec:negative}).
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews LLM-based RTL generation, physical-objective
optimisation, and the evaluation practices this work departs from.
Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the oracle, the frequency surrogate and its
safeguards, and the correctness-gated policy optimisation procedure.
Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the design families, the frozen held-out
protocol, and the measurement infrastructure.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the main results, the silicon measurements, the
mechanism analysis, the baselines, and the negative results.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses threats to validity and the limits of the
claims, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
